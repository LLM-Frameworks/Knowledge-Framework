# Fallacies to Look Out For

Working list built from practiced application. Not a textbook dump: every entry earned its place.

## The foundation: premises and conclusion

Every argument is a skeleton: **premises** (the reasons) leading to a **conclusion** (the claim). Two questions evaluate any argument:

1. Are the premises true?
2. Does the conclusion actually follow?

That's the whole game. Everything below is a way this goes wrong.

Most real arguments hide premises. Surfacing the hidden premise is half of fallacy-spotting.

## The first five (learn these cold)

- **Strawman** — Misrepresent the view, then defeat the misrepresentation.
- **Equivocation** — The same word, two meanings, one argument.
- **Ad hominem** — Attack the man, skip the argument.
- **Genetic fallacy** — Judge the claim by its origin.
- **Begging the question** — The conclusion hiding in the premises.

## Full list

1. **Self-refutation** — The claim kills itself. "There are no absolutes" (stated absolutely).

2. **Equivocation** — A word changes meaning mid-argument.

3. **Proof-texting** — A verse or quote ripped from context and deployed as a weapon.

4. **Guilt by association** — The association is real; the conclusion doesn't follow.

5. **Genetic fallacy** — Judging a claim by its source instead of its merit. Cuts both directions.

6. **Misplaced authority** — "Scholars say..." with no reasons attached. Credentials are not arguments.

7. **Strawman** — Attacking a version of the view nobody holds. Steel-man first.

8. **Steamroller / red herring** — Volume, rhetoric, and rabbit trails instead of an argument. Counter: one point at a time.

9. **Begging the question** — The conclusion smuggled into the premises or the definitions.

10. **Special pleading** — A standard applied to the other side but never to your own.

11. **Ad hominem** — Attack the arguer instead of the argument (including *tu quoque*).

12. **False dilemma** — Two options presented as the only options. Ask for the third.

13. **Appeal to ignorance** — "No evidence against it, so it's true" (or the reverse). Absence of evidence is not automatically evidence of absence — but when evidence *would be expected* if the claim were true and it is missing, that absence is evidence against the claim.

14. **No true Scotsman** — Identity goalpost-moving.

15. **Hasty generalization** — Anecdote laundered as data.

16. **Slippery slope** — A chain of dominoes with no links shown.

17. **Appeal to emotion** — Feelings deployed as premises.

18. **The fallacy fallacy** — "The argument was fallacious, therefore the conclusion is false." A bad argument can still carry a true conclusion.

19. **The snuck premise** — The conclusion smuggled into a *name* or label. The label does the persuading before the reasons are examined.

## Use rule

This list is a diagnostic instrument, not a weapon. Its job is finding the error so you can build the rebuttal — it is not the rebuttal. Never fallacy-shame. Diagnose privately; engage with questions.
