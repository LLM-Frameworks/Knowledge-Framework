# How to Have a Conversation or Debate Respectfully

The rules for respectful disagreement. Built from Koukl's *Tactics*, the Boghossian contrast, and practiced application.

## The foundation: respect

Respect the person — always, non-negotiably. They are never the enemy, never a project, never a patient. Their dignity doesn't depend on being right.

Their *views* get something different: a fair hearing, not deference. You don't have to treat every view as equally valid — that's not respect, it's abdication. What a view is owed: understand it before you answer it, represent it fairly, take it seriously enough to follow where it leads. Dismissal without understanding is the disrespect.

Demolishing a view respectfully is not a contradiction. It's the whole project: casting down imaginations, not people. (2 Cor 10:5)

## The aim

Truth, not victory. There are two kinds of questions: questions in service of truth and questions in service of doubt. This guide is for the first kind. If you're trying to win rather than to find what's true, put this down — you're doing something else.

If they legitimately have something, that's fine. That's the pursuit. A real point against you is not a loss — it's the thing you came for.

## The posture: ambassador

You are an ambassador, not an invader. An ambassador needs three things:

- **Knowledge** — the case. Know what you believe and why.
- **Wisdom** — tactics. Know how to engage without blowing the conversation up.
- **Character** — grace under fire. The person in front of you is never the enemy, even when they act like it.

## Part I — Rules of engagement

**1. Understand before you answer.** "What do you mean by that?" Most bad arguments die at the definition stage, and most heated debates are vocabulary disputes in disguise. Steelman first: state their view so fairly they'd sign it. Then check — literally ask: "Did I get that right?"

**2. Cards on the table.** State your own position. Answer the questions asked of you. The interrogator who hides his own beliefs while exposing yours isn't conversing — he's running an intervention.

**3. The burden stays with the claimant.** "How did you come to that conclusion?" You don't have to disprove every assertion dropped on the table. The one making the claim brings the reasons. When you make your claims, the burden is yours too.

**4. Stones, not slaps.** "Have you ever considered...?" One good question, placed gently, then silence. You don't have to win the conversation — plant something that works on them later.

**5. Diagnose privately.** The fallacy list is an internal instrument. It tells you where the argument breaks so you can build the rebuttal — it is not the rebuttal. Never fallacy-shame.

**6. The frame is part of the respect.** Conversation, not intervention. Peer, not patient.

**7. One point at a time.** When the steamroller starts, stop it. One point, addressed fully, or no conversation at all.

**8. Your job is the stone, not the conversion.** Plant the stone. Walk away. Sleep fine.

**9. You're allowed to pause.** "I'll have to think about that" is a good reply. Nobody owes an instant answer.

**10. Answer the heart, not just the letter.** Every point has a letter — the exact wording — and a heart — what the person actually means. Debate the heart. Attacking the letter when the heart is clear is a gotcha, not an argument.

**11. Flag the apparent contradiction; don't fire it.** When something looks contradictory, that's a prompt for clarification, not a conclusion. Check the underlying words before the contradiction counts as an objection.

## Part II — Rules for evaluating claims

**12. Authorities give opinions, not verdicts.** Every expert is an authority in their field — and an authority can only look at the evidence and offer a reasonable, defensible opinion. Citing a conclusion is fine; the trick is recognizing it *as* a conclusion. Weight the witness who goes against their own tribe: admission against interest is signal, not validation.

**13. Words are cheap. Actions come with a cost.** An opinion backed by cost is testimony — an opinion backed by nothing is noise. When someone's *actions* contradict their *words*, the words lose their witness without the argument losing its logic.

**14. The courtroom standard: reasonable doubt, tested witnesses.** It is not beyond a possible doubt — that's not the bar. It is beyond a reasonable doubt. Possible doubt is infinite. The four witness tests: (1) Were they really there? (2) Corroboration. (3) Consistency over time. (4) Bias / motive to lie.

## When it goes bad

- **Heat rising:** return to facts. If the facts don't cool it, walk away.
- **Bad faith:** you don't owe endless engagement to someone performing rather than conversing.
- **You're the problem:** if you feel the urge to humiliate rather than illuminate, stop.

## Worked example

Them: "The resurrection is just a legend that grew over time."

You: "What do you mean by legend?" *(Rule 1)*

Them: "The stories got exaggerated as they were retold. You know, telephone game."

You: "How did you come to that conclusion — what makes you think that's what happened here?" *(Rule 3)*
